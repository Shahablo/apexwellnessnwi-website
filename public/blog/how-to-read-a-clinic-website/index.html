Reading health information

The clinic website looks great. Who wrote it?

A clinic website can look expensive and still leave the important questions in eight-point type. The design may be lovely. The useful part is finding out who wrote the words, what supports them, and what has not been answered.

Here is a short way to read past the headline. Use it on our site, too.

By Apex Wellness Editorial Team · Published October 9, 2026

In this article

  • Find the people behind the words
  • Read the dates as labels
  • Follow the source to the actual statement
  • Notice when explanation turns into promotion
  • Keep the unanswered question visible

Find the people behind the words

An author credit identifies who prepared an article. A separate medical-review credit, when relevant, should identify who checked the specific version. A physician photo elsewhere on a site does not establish that the person reviewed the page you are reading.

Look for the reviewer’s name, relevant professional background, review date and explanation of what was reviewed. “Our medical team” may sound reassuring, but it does not answer those questions. An update about parking and an article about medication also need different kinds of scrutiny.

Read the dates as labels

Published, updated and medically reviewed are three different events. If a page was reviewed in May and rewritten in August, the August update does not tell you whether the revised wording received another review.

A copyright year in the footer is not an article review date. Neither is a future event mentioned in the text. Check the label attached to each date before assuming what it means.

Follow the source to the actual statement

Open a source link beside a claim. Does it discuss that particular point, or does it lead only to an organization’s homepage? Is the article’s statement narrower than the source, or much broader?

The National Library of Medicine’s MedlinePlus guide recommends checking who runs a site, why it exists, how information is reviewed, where claims come from and when content was written or updated. Those checks are more useful than a row of impressive-looking logos.

Notice when explanation turns into promotion

A clinic website also markets a business. That is not a secret, but it is worth noticing when a paragraph moves from explaining something to inviting you to sign up or buy. Does the evidence support that invitation, or just the mood of the page?

The National Center for Complementary and Integrative Health explains how advertising can borrow the appearance of independent news. That does not mean every commercial page is inaccurate. It means presentation alone cannot do the work of evidence.

Keep the unanswered question visible

Make a four-line note: the statement that caught your eye, its source, the author and review details, and what remains unclear. “I should ask who reviews this” is more useful than a vague feeling that a site seemed credible.
